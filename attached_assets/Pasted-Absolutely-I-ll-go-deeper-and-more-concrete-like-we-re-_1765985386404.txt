1. The generator is not RNG — it’s a constrained sampler

At a high level, the generator works like this:

Random selection → filtered by fit → weighted by board state → validated as a 3-piece set

So “randomness” is the input, not the final output.

⸻

2. Shape pool structure (more detail)

Internally, shapes are almost certainly grouped like this:

A. Safety primitives
	•	1×1
	•	1×2 (both orientations)
	•	Small 3-block L

Purpose:
	•	Patch micro-gaps
	•	Enable last-second clears

These have dynamic spawn weights, not fixed probabilities.

⸻

B. Structural builders
	•	1×3, 1×4
	•	2×2 square
	•	Medium L / T shapes

Purpose:
	•	Fill large regions
	•	Set up future clears

These dominate early–mid game.

⸻

C. Space stressors
	•	1×5
	•	3×3 square
	•	Asymmetric Z / extended L shapes

Purpose:
	•	Consume board real estate
	•	Test spatial discipline

These are rare early, more common late.

⸻

3. Board state signals (what the generator “sees”)

The generator likely computes a lightweight feature vector each turn:
	•	empty_cells
	•	max_empty_region_size
	•	hole_count (isolated 1–2 cell gaps)
	•	almost_complete_lines (rows/cols at 6–7/8 filled)
	•	edge_density vs center density

This is cheap to compute and hugely informative.

⸻

4. Piece weighting is contextual, not linear

Instead of “hard rules,” the generator adjusts weights.

Example (conceptual):

if empty_cells < 18:
  weight(1x1) += high
  weight(1x2) += medium
  weight(3x3) -= large

But importantly:
	•	It never sets weights to zero
	•	Even “bad” pieces can still appear

This preserves unpredictability.

⸻

5. Three-piece batch construction (critical nuance)

The generator doesn’t just pick 3 pieces at once.

More likely:
	1.	Pick piece A (context-weighted)
	2.	Simulate placements of A
	3.	Pick piece B that:
	•	fits somewhere after A
	•	doesn’t eliminate all options
	4.	Pick piece C that:
	•	fits in at least one permutation of A/B

This is why order matters — the system guarantees existence, not clarity.

⸻

6. Solvability ≠ generosity

The generator ensures:
	•	There exists some valid sequence

It does not ensure:
	•	It’s obvious
	•	It preserves long-term board health
	•	It avoids fragmentation

This is why mistakes feel subtle and delayed.

⸻

7. Anti-pattern avoidance (very important)

The system actively avoids:
	•	Three huge blocks in one batch
	•	Three identical shapes
	•	Three pieces that only fit in one exact spot

These would feel unfair or deterministic.

So there’s likely a diversity constraint:
	•	Different footprint sizes
	•	At least one compact piece
	•	At least one line-capable piece

⸻

8. Difficulty ramp is entropy-based, not time-based

There is no “level 10 = harder pieces.”

Instead:
	•	The longer you play well
	•	The more entropy your board accumulates
	•	The fewer “perfect fits” remain
	•	The more the generator can legally give you stressor shapes

You are slowly tightening your own noose.

⸻

9. Why it feels personal

The generator is reactive enough that:
	•	Two players at the same score
	•	Can receive totally different piece sets

Because the board state, not score, is the input.

This creates the illusion of:

“The game knows how I play”

In reality, it knows how your board looks.

⸻

10. The killer design choice

The most important decision:

The generator never fixes your board for you.

It gives you tools, not solutions.
You still have to:
	•	choose order
	•	choose sacrifice zones
	•	choose when to clear vs store space

That’s why high scores feel earned.

TL;DR (generator deep spec)
	•	Context-weighted piece pool
	•	Board-feature driven probabilities
	•	Sequential 3-piece validation
	•	Guaranteed short-term solvability
	•	No long-term safety guarantees
	•	Difficulty emerges from entropy, not levels
